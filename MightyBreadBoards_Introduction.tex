Urban air monitoring must capture pollution changes over time and across various locations to help identify pollution problems and mitigate adverse health effects. While government monitoring stations are highly accurate, they are expensive and cannot be universally deployed. Low-cost smart sensors can be distributed more widely, but their readings fluctuate due to temperature, humidity, and sensor aging. Machine learning models, such as Random Forest and Support Vector Regression, are frequently used to correct these inaccuracies. However, these models struggle to predict extreme pollution levels that exceed their training data. This study addresses this limitation by developing a system that utilizes sensors alongside a novel ensemble modeling approach. This new approach enables the system to account for environmental factors while accurately predicting high pollution levels.
\section{Background of the Study}
\label{sec: back}
Ambient air pollution is known around the world as an environmental danger and a serious health problem. Being around pollutants like fine particles (PM2.5, PM10) and harmful gases such as nitrogen dioxide (NO2) ozone (O3) carbon monoxide (CO) and sulfur dioxide (SO2) leads to many early deaths each year and creates big financial problems globally \cite{Concas_2021,WorldHealthOrganization_2026a}. Officials use fixed air quality monitoring stations with methods to check if rules are followed and to look at health risks \cite{Giordano_2021,WorldHealthOrganization_2026b}. These proper tools are very accurate. Cost a lot to buy and keep running. This means there are not stations and each one covers a large area, sometimes several square kilometers \cite{Concas_2021,Hayward_2024}. Because of that these systems miss areas where pollution is very high and also miss the small changes in pollution levels that happen in busy cities \cite{Concas_2021,Giordano_2021}

To fix the problem of not covering areas low-cost sensors have become a good choice. These sensors are cheaper and smaller making it possible to have more of them for coverage and to track personal exposure \cite{Concas_2021}. Even though they can cover places they measure air directly and have big errors \cite{Giordano_2021,Hayward_2024}. When measuring particles optical sensors guess the weight from how light bounces off them. This guess is wrong if the particles are not all the size or shape \cite{Giordano_2021}. High humidity makes the particles bigger which tricks the sensors into thinking there is pollution than there really is \cite{Giordano_2021,Hayward_2024}. When measuring gases the sensors use reactions that change with temperature. This causes them to mix up signals from gases especially between NO2 and O3 \cite{Hayward_2024}. Also as the sensors age and are used more their readings change over time \cite{Concas_2021,Hayward_2024}. 

Since the numbers from the sensors are not close to the values they must be checked against the proper instruments in the same place \cite{Concas_2021,Hayward_2024}. Simple models like linear regression are often used because they are easy. They can't handle the complicated and changing relationships between the sensor data, the weather and other air chemicals \cite{Hayward_2024}. Some advanced models like Random Forest, Support Vector Regression and Artificial Neural Networks have been used to better deal with these issues but each has its own problems \cite{Concas_2021,Hayward_2024}. Random Forest can't predict when pollution is higher, than what it was trained on. It gives wrong numbers when pollution is very bad \cite{Hayward_2024}. Support Vector Regression needs the settings and doesn't work well with time-based data \cite{Concas_2021,Hayward_2024}. Artificial Neural Networks need a lot of data. Use too much power \cite{Concas_2021,Hayward_2024}. Studies show that no one model works best for all types of pollution and different weather \cite{Concas_2021,Hayward_2024}.
\section{Problem Statement}
Although non linear models like Random Forest and Artificial Neural Networks are widely used to calibrate low cost air quality sensors, they struggle to extrapolate. Decision tree models like Random Forest cannot predict values outside their training limits because their predictions rely on averaging values at the leaf nodes. Similarly, Neural Networks focus on the most common baseline readings, causing them to under predict rare peak concentrations. Because these models cannot extrapolate beyond historical data, they flatten or cut off high concentration spikes, making them unreliable for real time health warnings during severe pollution events.

\section{Purpose of the Study}
The purpose of this study seeks to benefit the following:
\subsection{The Public}
Empowers communities by providing accessible, street-scale air quality information that captures the spatial and temporal variations of pollutants across urban areas. Access to this real-time data raises public awareness of localized pollution hotspots and personal exposure, aiding individuals in making informed decisions to protect themselves from respiratory health risks.
\subsection{Government and Regulatory Bodies}
Assists environmental agencies by providing a scalable, cost-effective method to supplement expensive, sparse traditional monitoring stations. The integration of dense sensor networks allows for high-resolution mapping of urban air quality, helping policymakers target specific emission sources and rapidly assess the impact of local emission control interventions.
\subsection{Academe and Future Researchers}
Offers high-resolution, long-term datasets necessary for advanced modeling and analysis, particularly in resource-constrained or under-monitored settings. It provides a robust foundation for future environmental research by demonstrating the real-world application of machine learning techniques for in-field sensor calibration and drift compensation.
\subsection{ECE Field}
Advances the development of physical hardware reliability by implementing real-time drift correction and machine learning models directly on edge devices. This research contributes to optimizing memory footprints and computational efficiency for microcontrollers, reducing reliance on cloud-based processing for autonomous sensor nodes. 
\subsection{SDG 3: Good Health}
Supports public health initiatives by delivering accurate data on human exposure to harmful particulate matter (PM1, PM2.5, and PM10), which is linked to millions of premature deaths globally due to respiratory and cardiovascular diseases. Accurate monitoring helps mitigate these risks by enabling timely health advisories.
\subsection{SDG 9: Innovation}
Fosters technological innovation by developing and refining cost-effective IoT sensing platforms paired with robust machine learning calibration models. This enhances the global capability to deploy intelligent, autonomous environmental monitoring infrastructure.
\subsection{SDG 11: Cities}
Enables the creation of pervasive, high-density wireless sensor networks that are essential for smart city development. By providing ubiquitous monitoring capabilities, urban planners can better identify industrial pollution clusters and protect residential areas, fostering healthier urban environments.
\subsection{SDG 13: Climate}
Strengthens resilience and adaptive capacity to climate-related hazards by continuously monitoring environmental parameters and establishing a localized evidence base for pollution management. 
\subsection{SDG 17: Partners}
Encourages collaborative frameworks between academic institutions, government bodies, and local communities. By incorporating citizen science and open data sharing, the project helps democratize environmental data and overcome resource constraints in global air quality monitoring.
